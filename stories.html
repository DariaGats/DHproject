Three stories read closely

“The Cop and the Anthem”

In “The Cop and the Anthem,” Soapy has no home, and he wants to be arrested so that he can spend the winter in prison. He tries again and again, and nothing happens. Then he decides to change his life, and at that moment he is arrested. The last paragraph has only 15 words, and the typical paragraph has 41: “Three months on the Island,” said the Magistrate in the Police Court the next morning. The surprise comes in paragraph 44 of 48, so the ending only gives the sentence. The story passes one test out of three, but the reversal is so clear that I count it as a twist.

“Memoirs of a Yellow Dog”

In “Memoirs of a Yellow Dog,” the dog tells the story himself. At the end his master insults him with a long list of names and then calls him “Pete,” and the dog is so happy that five tails would not be enough. The last paragraph is shorter than the typical one (27 words against 51), but its sentence is long: 27 words, while the sentences in the rest of the story have 18.8. I find no twist here, because nothing in the ending changes how we understand the story. A short ending is not always a surprise.

“By Courier”

In “By Courier,” a man and a woman are not speaking, because after the scene in the conservatory she doubts him. The turning point comes earlier, in paragraph 23 of 27. A note of gratitude appears, and it shows that he is a doctor giving first aid. The scene that made her doubt him was not a betrayal. The last paragraph has 19 words, and the typical paragraph has 55: “Tell that guy on the other bench,” she said, with a happy, tremulous laugh, “that his girl wants him.” So the ending is not the surprise, it is the answer. If I measured only the last paragraph, I would see the reconciliation and miss the twist.
